# The Person Who Remembers Your Kid's Name Is Worth More Than the Person With 5,000 LinkedIn Connections

Most people walk into a room full of strangers and start hunting. You can see it in their eyes. They scan badges, calculate relevance, and move toward the highest-status person they can reach. They collect. Business cards, LinkedIn accepts, brief handshakes with a mental note that says *might be useful later.*

I have watched this happen roughly 20,000 times.

Since September 2008, I have hosted IMParty, Austin's largest entrepreneur networking event, every single month. Never missed one. Seventeen years of watching people enter rooms, work rooms, and leave rooms. Somewhere north of $2 billion in transactions have come from introductions made through those gatherings. And here is the thing that pattern taught me, the thing I am now certain about: the people responsible for almost all of that value behave nothing like the people you would expect.

The collectors look impressive. The connectors look interested.

That is the entire framework. But let me make it specific, because frameworks only matter when they change behavior.

**A collector** tracks quantity. They measure a night's success by how many new contacts they added. Their follow-up is a templated LinkedIn message within 24 hours. They remember what you do. They forget what you said.

**A connector** tracks resonance. They measure a night's success by whether they created one introduction that made both people's eyes light up. Their follow-up is a text three weeks later that says, "I kept thinking about what you said about your daughter's science project. My friend runs a STEM nonprofit. Want an intro?" They remember what matters to you. What you do is just context.

The behavioral gap between these two is not subtle. It is a canyon. And it shows up in three places you can observe tonight, at your next dinner, in your next conversation.

**Where their eyes go.** Collectors look past you while you are talking. Not dramatically. Just a flicker. They are scanning for the next conversation, the better conversation, the one that might yield more. Connectors lock in. Not in an intense, uncomfortable way. In a way that makes you feel like the room just got smaller and that is somehow a good thing.

**What they ask.** Collectors ask what you do, how long you have been doing it, and where they can find you online. Connectors ask what you are stuck on, what you are excited about, and what problem keeps you up. The first set of questions sorts you into a category. The second set discovers who you actually are.

**What they do with the information.** This is where the canyon becomes uncrossable. A collector stores your details in a CRM and tags you for future outreach. A connector holds a loose, living map of problems and people in their head, and when a match surfaces, they act on it. Not because it benefits them. Because the match exists and leaving it unmade feels like waste.

Let me tell you about a night in 2016 that I still think about.

Two founders came to IMParty the same month. One had built a SaaS company doing around $4 million in revenue. The other had just left a corporate job to start something new, no revenue, no product, just conviction and a half-formed idea. The SaaS founder was a collector. Worked the room efficiently, hit every cluster of people, left with a stack of contacts. The new founder was a connector. She spent 40 minutes talking to one person. A designer. Not a potential investor, not a strategic partner. A designer who had mentioned, almost in passing, that he was burned out and thinking about leaving his agency.

Three weeks later she introduced that designer to another founder she had met at a previous event who desperately needed design leadership but could not afford agency rates. That introduction became a partnership. That partnership became a product. That product, five years later, sold for a number that would make the SaaS founder's $4 million look like a rounding error.

The SaaS founder, by the way, is still at roughly $4 million. His network is wide. It is also shallow. And shallow networks do not compound.

This is the part that most people miss: connecting is not a strategy. It is a disposition. You cannot perform it. Collectors who try to act like connectors come off as manipulative because the underlying orientation is still extractive. The question running beneath every conversation is still "what can this person do for me?" and people feel that. Every single time.

Connectors operate from a different question entirely: "What do I now know that someone else needs to know?"

That question turns your memory into an engine. Every conversation becomes fuel for a future introduction you have not thought of yet. You stop networking and start noticing. You stop collecting and start carrying people with you.

I built my entire life on this distinction. Founders Lounge, my curated monthly dinner for founders running $2 million to $200 million companies, works because every seat is chosen by a connector's logic, not a collector's. The question is never "who is impressive?" The question is "who needs to be in the same room right now, and why?"

My identity line is this: I know everyone. And I can count them on two hands.

That is not a contradiction. It is the whole point. Twenty thousand entrepreneurs have walked through my events. The ones I truly know, the ones who changed my life and whose lives I changed, are a small number. But those relationships are so deep, so layered with mutual understanding and accumulated trust, that they reach everywhere. Deep networks are not smaller than wide ones. They are larger. They just grow underground, like root systems, invisible until something blooms.

You are one of these two people. You already know which one.

The harder question is whether you are willing to stop counting and start remembering.
